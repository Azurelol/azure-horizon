# Battle

<p>You can tell a lot what a game system cares about by the degree of how much ink it spends writing on what.</p><p>This system cares about combat and has formalized rules for how they are run. It does this because I believe they are exciting part of the genre, can and should be fun to engage with and allow both players and the director a relief valve to let loose and play with each one's toys and doodads.</p><p>Still, having the right mindset is in order when it comes to running them. In this section I will provide some principles to keep in mind when designing and running encounters in the system.</p><h2>Difficulty</h2><p>You are not there to throw down a gauntlet and challenge your players directly in a fair contest. In fact, you <strong>want</strong> to lose by having the PCs overcome whatever foes you throw their way.</p><p>You just want it to be interesting and fun to do so.</p><h2>Tactical Depth</h2><p>Designing interesting encounters is hard. We have to design our adversaries so that they are interesting to engage with, but with easily telegraphed mechanics and routines so that players can learn and overcome them.</p><p>One of the easiest pitfalls can be making enemies too simple, such that players quickly figure them out then just execute a set rotation on each of their turns with no variance.</p><p>This is what I consider a "solved" encounter where the players are just running through the paces and just rolling dice. Now, it's not to say that such encounters are not pleasant in their own way once in a while but they should not be the norm.</p><p>But they can be boring both for you and your players. Just like in the overall narrative of your story, you and your players should want to be surprised and excited by what is happening.</p><p>How can we do so? We introduce variance whenever we can. This can come from random targeting, randomness in the abilities the adversaries can perform, even random changes in the arena.</p><p>Last of all when you design encounters, you should keep all of this in mind and attempt to simulate what your players will do on their characters when you run it. You are the one most familiar with their capabilities and the one most suited to understand their strengths and weaknesses.</p>

# Introduction

<p>This section covers both advice and instructions on how to run campaigns with this system in a structured, procedural way that gives you room for both you and your players to be creative. </p><p>Your role as the first among equals in your collaborative story-telling is to provide the opportunity for your players to express themselves and engage themselves with the fiction and the game’s systems.</p>

# Campaign Structure

<p>While the system is designed for long-term play, I recommend planning out your campaign in terms of narrative arcs which naturally arise during play based on what the player characters do, what their motivations are and what the threats are.</p><ul><li><p>Session Zero: In this session you will sit down with your players and create your setting in a collaborative exercise, as well as the player characters that will reside in it. </p></li><li><p>Prologue: </p></li><li><p>Episode: </p></li><li><p>Epilogue:</p></li></ul><p>An arc should be roughly 8-12 sessions in length ending with a satisfying conclusion which can leave room for more if the table wishes to continue adventuring in the setting, with their current characters or new ones!</p><p>It is best to plan one session ahead at a time so that your campaign’s plot is truly reactive. It also helps prevent you from trying to guide players down your prescribed path that you envisioned.</p><p>The instinct to guide your players down the paths you lay down is one you will have to be constantly vigilant against throughout your campaign. Players are already primed from other experiences in this medium and closely related ones to listen to what your NPCs ask of them and treat it as instructions to follow.</p>

# Session Zero

<p>Your campaign will come together after you have gathered your group of players and run a session zero by going through the Setting creation process of your campaign.</p><p>I would not expect your players to finish building their PCs by the end of your session zero. Instead you will be wanting to finish their character concepts and building pre-existing relationships between their characters.</p>

# Episode

<p>An episode is defined as a session where the narrative advances in a meaningful way. These are the meat and bones of your campaigns, the bulk of your sessions.</p><h2>Running an Episode</h2><p>Over the years I found the following structure to serve me well when it came to running my sessions in a timely manner for my fellow humans. </p><ol><li><p>Engage in random banter as people trickle in around the start time. Try to get a feel for your player’s mood here. Sometimes someone could be having a bad day or on the contrary is really excited and could use a bone or two. </p></li><li><p>Once everyone’s in, ask a random player for a recap of the events of your last session, correcting any misremembered events as non-pedantically as you possibly can.</p></li><li><p>If possible, start the session with a villain scene to highlight the progress of the villains or just keep them in your player’s consciousness.</p><p>Do the thing.</p></li><li><p>As you end the session before letting everyone scamper away, I strongly recommend going around the table and gathering feedback from your players on how the session went and to get a general pulse on the table. If you are at a narrative crossroads of sorts feel free to ask them what they think their PCs will do next, which will help you prepare accordingly.</p></li></ol>

# Best Practices

<h2>Narrate your actions during conflicts</h2><p>In a system without grids and without having to nitpick distances and ranges, we are freed from much of what can eat up a player’s processing time when it comes to planning out their turn.</p><p>Both you and your players should be making use of this in order to be more proactive when it comes to narrating what your character does on their turn. </p><p>Instead of saying “I… uh.  use SHADOWSTRIKE on the mook“, you could say “Shadowfang rushes forward, his blade clad in darkness as he swings it down on the mook.”</p><p>You can think of each check to perform an action as a mechanism to decide who gets to narrate what happens. If the attacker succeeds their check, they can describe how they landed their attack. If they fail it, then their target gets to narrate their defensive action. </p><p>In this way the conflict narration is a back and forth between GM and players. A little bit of flavor in your conflicts will enhance the experience for everyone involved.</p><h2>Game Balance is everyone’s responsibility</h2><p>The system expects GMs to tailor encounters and adversaries to their party which is predicated on the strength and weaknesses of the PCs your players have made. This is a tall order for any designer let a GM that is learning the system or with a small amount of time each week to design interesting encounters.</p><p>The system does not strictly balance its character options like, let's say, Pathfinder 2E, and gives players a lot of possible synergies, which in some combinations can sadly end up trivializing certain conflict types or possibly break your conflicts in half. You should be honest about this and invite your players to work with you when it comes to picking out their character options.</p><p>This system is not meant to be a you-versus-them affair where the players try to make the most powerful characters they can in order to survive your dungeon and show you up. This is a narrative, fiction-first system! Where their characters are free to take more risks and to occasionally lose. It’s okay to fail!</p><p>What your players should be thinking when building their characters is instead to consider what would be interesting to play, in terms of options and possible synergies with their fellow players. Creating interesting characters with clear strengths and weaknesses (both in personality and mechanics) is way more interesting for the narrative, but also gives the GM a clearly delineated design space which they can use to craft interesting encounters which are interesting to engage with.</p>

# Common Pitfalls

<h2>Bad Pacing</h2><p>Have you ever read a book that you found boring and abandoned partway? Have you read another you felt you couldn’t put down?</p><p>Few of us are well-versed in literary or media criticism but all of us can tell when we are engaged on something and when it starts to lose our attention. Recall fiction you have read with overly-flowery prose or way-too-detailed descriptions of things. Recall side plots that went nowhere, did not push the narrative meaningfully or were interesting on themselves. Recall scenes that could have been summarized without anything of value being lost.</p><p>The same can be true of your game sessions. You and your players only have so much time and energy to go around and you should not easily squander it. An alternative name for a gamemaster in some systems is that of director. (Hence why I chose it for this system). I believe it fits very well in the kind of approach you should be taking with your sessions. </p><p>One of your main imperatives should be to keep the story moving forward and keep both yourself and your players interested. </p><ul><li><p>When to hurry things up along and end a scene, or when to let it go on for just a bit longer. </p></li><li><p>When it’s worth introducing an NPC and bringing up a dialogue with your players, or when that interaction could just quickly be summarized, perhaps with a check.</p></li><li><p>When it’s time to introduce a conflict to break up a narrative-heavy session, or do the inverse and give the players a chance to wind down.</p></li><li><p>When something is worth using the conflict subsystem, or when it’s fine with just a single check or not even.</p></li></ul><p>It can be a lot, but continuous practice, honest feedback from your players and a degree of self-awareness will serve you here.</p><h2>Pointless conflicts</h2><p>Easy encounters by design consume minimal resources for the player characters and are not meant to be particularly challenging. So before you run one of them, they should accomplish one of the following:</p><ul><li><p>Introduce a narrative beat, tell you something new about the location or mark a shift in the current narrative.</p></li><li><p>Foreshadow and teach a new mechanic which will be used by an upcoming villain.</p></li><li><p>Provide an avenue to play out a power fantasy for the players. This could happen by introducing a previously threatening former enemy again, which would now prove trivial to the PCs.</p></li><li><p>Break up a long stretch of narrative and let your players stretch their legs a bit, so to speak.</p></li></ul><h3>Presentation over fundamentals</h3><p>It is a seductive lure.  The genre this system emulates is well known for its games and media which are highlighted by its high production values and visuals.</p><p>Once you realize that with enough effort Foundry can animate the characters on screen or that you can build impressive menus on screen and in general emulate what you have seen in your favorite games, there is the danger of going overboard with it. The danger of going the extra mile. Don’t. </p><p>At least not at first, and always with a measure of self-restraint.</p><p>Before you do so, you should ask yourself whether the time spent doing something your players most likely will find novel and “cool” at best would be better spent on working on the design of your adversaries, your campaign’s treasure pool, possible upcoming story beats (based on what the PCs are up to) and so on.</p><p>And most of all, it’s about pacing yourself. Don’t burn yourself out and lose your passion for the game!</p><h2>Boring Equipment</h2><p>One of the sources of engagement for players during TTRPG play is character progression, which can come intrinsically (level ups!) or extrinsically (outside sources such as equipment). </p><p>When designing items for your campaign and your PCs you should avoid giving just numerical bonuses and instead think of items that are interesting to use. You should avoid giving them anything but minor (+1) numerical bonuses lest you break the math and in my opinion entirely avoid items that give blanket immunities or resistances without interesting drawbacks.</p><h2>Punishing Checks</h2><p>Just because you can ask for rolls at any prompt from the players doesn’t mean you should. If your player has a good idea and wants their PC to do something cool or interesting with little to no narrative consequence, you can just… let their PCs do the action. Even embellish it yourself!</p><p>You want to reward your players for being engaged and proactive, and failing to perform basic actions their PCs are narratively equipped to do will surely put a damper on that.</p><h2>Not letting go of your preconceptions</h2><p>If you and your players came from other, more traditional systems and have been playing them for many years you will have picked up what I consider are… bad habits. Which came from the play patterns those systems provided and that you all got used to.</p><p>It is time to let go.</p><p>For directors this can come down to wanting to control the narrative with an iron grip and have a clear idea in mind of how the story will develop. For players, this can come down to a form of passive play arising out of a lack of agency over the narrative. You ever wonder why players obsess over their character builds? Could it perhaps be because they don’t get to really make other meaningful choices during play?</p><p>While there’s a lot to say about this particular topic, I will stop now with this much: the fact that you got this far in wanting to play the system means you are ready for something new and more likely, want your stories to feel like those you have seen and played through the many inspirations the system has. </p><p>So will your players. So why not tell them together?</p>

# Checks

<p>When prompting the players to make a check, they must be informed of the consequences for their success or failure before the roll. In this way as a group you commit yourselves to follow through.</p><p>Always have you and your players make all checks in the open.</p>
